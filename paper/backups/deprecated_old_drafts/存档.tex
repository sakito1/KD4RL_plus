\begin{abstract}
Financial markets exhibit pronounced non-stationarity, conditional heteroskedasticity, and structural regime shifts, making it challenging for single-layer reinforcement learning (RL) models to simultaneously achieve long-term stability and short-term adaptability. Existing RL-based portfolio management approaches typically rely on fixed rebalancing intervals and rigid weight structures, lacking explicit modeling of regime transition risks and the ability to dynamically extract residual Alpha under varying volatility conditions. As a result, fixed holding horizons and static allocation schemes often lead to delayed risk responses during trend reversals and insufficient profit amplification during trend acceleration.
To address these limitations, we propose \textit{SentinelAlpha}, a sentinel-driven regime-aware hierarchical reinforcement learning framework. The proposed architecture explicitly models the probability of upward trend persistence and introduces a threshold hysteresis-based risk gating mechanism to structurally decouple risk supervision from return optimization. Meanwhile, a frequency-decomposed hierarchical RL design coordinates long-horizon Beta allocation with short-horizon Alpha refinement, enhancing allocation flexibility and improving risk-adjusted performance.
Specifically, \textit{SentinelAlpha} consists of:
(1) an input-dependent hierarchical state-space model (HD-SSM) that estimates asset-level upward probabilities;
(2) a probability hysteresis sentinel module (PHS) implementing portfolio-level RESET and LOCK mechanisms via threshold risk control rules; and
(3) a frequency-separated hierarchical RL architecture in which the Outer layer optimizes low-frequency Beta allocation, while the Inner layer explores high-frequency residual Alpha within a constrained convex subspace.
Empirical results on the Nasdaq-100 and CSI-300 benchmarks demonstrate that \textit{SentinelAlpha} consistently outperforms multiple RL baselines and traditional portfolio strategies in terms of Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, and return stability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%%%%% 1. 文献综述（合并到intro） %%%%%
Portfolio Management (PM) is fundamentally a combinatorial optimization problem that aims to maximize risk-adjusted returns through dynamic asset weight allocation under controlled risk exposure. Classical financial theories such as the Markowitz mean–variance framework  \citep{MVO}. and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)  \citep{CAMP} are grounded in static or single-period decision assumptions. However, real-world financial markets exhibit pronounced non-stationarity, structural breaks, and conditional heteroskedasticity, rendering portfolio management a continuous sequential decision process. In practice, an investment strategy must simultaneously address two coupled questions: (i) \textbf{When to rebalance?}  (ii) \textbf{How to allocate capital?} 
% This form of horizontal coordination has demonstrated remarkable effectiveness across various industries, such as blood bank in healthcare systems \citep{shokouhifar2021inventory} and perishable goods in retail networks \citep{wei2022dynamic}, etc. 
Early research on dynamic asset allocation has shown that in multi-period or continuous-time settings, failure to adapt to evolving market conditions leads to significant risk exposure mismatch.\citep{Perold1988DynamicSF}

Recent advances in reinforcement learning (RL) provide a unified end-to-end framework for portfolio decision-making. PGPortfolio \citep{4} applies policy gradient methods to weight generation; FinRL \citep{5} systematizes RL-based trading pipelines; DeepTrader \citep{6} incorporates market condition embeddings to enhance risk–return balance. Meanwhile, modeling cross-asset relationships and improving interpretability have become central themes. AlphaStock \cite{alphastock} leverages cross-asset attention to capture relative strength relationships, while Investor-Imitator \citep{8} enhances policy expressiveness through behavior imitation.

Despite these advances, most RL-based portfolio frameworks suffer from two structural limitations.First, \textbf{fixed rebalancing intervals}.Strategies are typically executed at predefined periodic frequencies \citep{4,5,6}, leading to delayed risk responses during regime shifts and amplified losses during market downturns.Second, \textbf{rigid weight structures}.Many methods generate portfolio weights only at the beginning of a holding period, with minimal adjustment thereafter. A single policy network is tasked with simultaneously modeling long-term trends and short-term volatility within one time scale, making it difficult to balance return stability and risk control.

We propose the following structural hypothesis:In non-stationary environments, a portfolio strategy must exhibit both temporal flexibility and allocation flexibility.

Temporal flexibility allows holding horizons to be dynamically determined by market conditions, while allocation flexibility enables rapid residual adjustments around a stable baseline configuration.

Based on this hypothesis, we propose SentinelAlpha, a structurally decomposed framework consisting of three modules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trend Probability Model}, which estimates continuous downside probability flows rather than discrete directional labels;
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Portfolio Holding Sentinel (PHS)}, which employs a threshold hysteresis mechanism to trigger RESET or LOCK operations, thereby dynamically determining holding durations;
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning Architecture}, where the Outer policy optimizes a low-frequency baseline allocation (Beta), and the Inner policy performs constrained residual adjustments to capture short-term Alpha.
\end{itemize}

This decomposition explicitly separates risk detection, long-term allocation, and short-term refinement.The key advantage of this design lies in transforming risk control from a continuous penalty embedded in the reward function into an exogenous structural boundary in the decision process. Rather than indirectly learning risk constraints through gradient signals, the policy search is restricted to a controlled subspace. Consequently, the framework improves robustness under non-stationary and low signal-to-noise environments while maintaining responsiveness to market changes.

We summarize our contributions as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trend Probability Flow Modeling}.We propose a segmented soft-label construction method and train a causal probability predictor that outputs continuous probability flows. Compared with single-step classification, this approach mitigates sparse supervision and provides intensity information for fine-grained gating.
    \item \textbf{Rule-Based Sentinel Mechanis}m.A threshold portfolio gating module that operates independently of gradient updates, enhancing interpretability and verifiability of risk boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Beta–Alpha Decomposition}.Portfolio returns are decomposed into baseline (Beta) and residual (Alpha) components, optimized at different frequency scales under convex constraints to reduce search dimensionality.
    \item \textbf{Structural Decoupling of Temporal and Allocation Flexibility}.Exit timing and weight adjustment are explicitly separated, alleviating multi-scale objective conflicts in non-stationary environments.
    \item \textbf{Probability-Gated Structural Stability}.By placing risk constraints outside policy gradient optimization, policy search is conducted within a controlled subspace, improving practical stability and interpretability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Classical Portfolio Optimization and Heuristic Rebalancing}
Modern portfolio theory is rooted in the mean--variance optimization framework\citep{MVO}, while the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) provides an equilibrium-based interpretation of risk pricing\citep{CAMP}. These classical approaches typically rely on distributional stationarity or single-period assumptions, which limit their applicability under non-stationary financial time series with structural regime shifts. As a result, heuristic rebalancing rules are frequently adopted in practice.
Research on dynamic asset allocation has shown that fixed rebalancing rules may lead to risk exposure mismatch when market conditions change\citep{Perold1988DynamicSF}. Opportunistic rebalancing further introduces condition-triggered paradigms, emphasizing optimal trade-offs between deviation thresholds and transaction costs to avoid unnecessary turnover\citep{9}. Meanwhile, transaction costs play a critical role in portfolio optimization and must be incorporated into the decision process in a robust manner\citep{10}.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Portfolio Management}
Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a sequential decision-making framework for portfolio management. Early works such as PGPortfolio demonstrate the feasibility of end-to-end weight generation via policy gradient methods\citep{4}, while FinRL systematizes RL-based trading and portfolio management into a reusable toolkit\citep{5}.
In terms of risk control and cost modeling, several studies incorporate maximum drawdown and other downside risk measures into the learning objective\citep{11}, and systematically investigate the joint modeling of transaction costs and risk control\citep{23}. Cost-sensitive deep RL frameworks have also been proposed to explicitly account for trading frictions in portfolio learning\citep{24}.
From a structural perspective, DeepTrader enhances robustness under extreme market conditions by embedding market state representations\citep{6}, while methods such as HADAPS explore hierarchical and multi-asset adaptive portfolio learning architectures\citep{25}. Regarding interpretability and cross-asset relationship modeling, AlphaStock integrates cross-asset attention with interpretability analysis to capture relative strength relationships\citep{alphastock}. In terms of knowledge integration and exploration, Investor-Imitator enhances policy learning via imitation learning and knowledge extraction mechanisms\citep{8}.

\section{Problem Formulation}
Consider a regime-aware portfolio management system with a universe of $N$ tradable assets $\mathcal{X}=\{x_1,x_2,\cdots,x_N\}$. The portfolio is operated by a central controller whose goal is to maximize long-term risk-adjusted performance under transaction costs. A trading horizon is divided into a set of $T$ trading days $\mathcal{T}=\{1,2,\cdots,T\}$. At each trading day $t\in\mathcal{T}$ (after market close), the controller observes a composite market state $s_t=(s_t^{out}, s_t^{inn}, p_t)$, where $s_t^{out}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\tau_{out}\times F}$ and $s_t^{inn}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\tau_{inn}\times F}$ denote long-horizon and short-horizon feature, respectively, and $p_t=(p_{1,t},\cdots,p_{N,t})$ is the vector of asset-level uptrend probabilities produced by the regime model. The controller outputs an execution weight vector $\mathbf{w}_t\in\mathbb{R}^N$ satisfying the simplex constraints $w_{i,t}\ge 0$ and $\sum_{i=1}^N w_{i,t}=1$.

To describe price dynamics, we define the close-to-close price relative vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_t=\left[\frac{p^{close}_{1,t+1}}{p^{close}_{1,t}},\cdots,\frac{p^{close}_{N,t+1}}{p^{close}_{N,t}}\right]^\top,
\end{equation}
where $p^{close}_{i,t}$ denotes the closing price of asset $x_i$ at time $t$. Given the previous execution weights $\mathbf{w}_{t-1}$, the pre-trade (drifted) weights at time $t$ are determined by market movement as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}^{drift}_t=\frac{\mathbf{y}_{t-1}\odot \mathbf{w}_{t-1}}{\mathbf{y}_{t-1}^\top \mathbf{w}_{t-1}}.
\end{equation}
Similarly, the baseline portfolio also admits a drifted form $\mathbf{b}^{drift}_t$.

To avoid fixed rebalancing intervals, we introduce an exogenous probability gate that determines whether the system should trigger a rebalancing decision. Let $g_t\in\{0,1\}$ denote the gating action, where $g_t=1$ indicates \emph{Switch} and $g_t=0$ indicates \emph{Hold}. The gate is driven by the probability-weighted portfolio signal $p_t^{held}=\sum_{i=1}^N w^{drift}_{i,t}p_{i,t}$. We apply a hysteresis rule with thresholds $\tau_{low}<\tau_{high}$:
\begin{equation}
g_t=
\begin{cases}
1,& p_t^{held}<\tau_{low},\\
0,& \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The gate acts as an exogenous risk boundary and is not optimized via gradients.

When $g_t=1$, the Outer policy produces a candidate baseline portfolio $\mathbf{b}^{cand}_t=\pi_{out}(s_t)$ with $\mathbf{b}^{cand}_t\in\Delta^N$. The effective baseline weights are then given by $\mathbf{b}^{used}_t=\mathbf{b}^{cand}_t$ if $g_t=1$, and $\mathbf{b}^{used}_t=\mathbf{b}^{drift}_t$ otherwise. On top of the baseline, the Inner policy outputs an adjustment direction $\mathbf{w}^{chg}_t\in\Delta^N$, and the execution weights are computed via a convex combination
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}_t=(1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{b}^{used}_t+\alpha_t \mathbf{w}^{chg}_t,
\quad \alpha_t\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
which constrains tactical deviations around the strategic baseline.

Rebalancing incurs transaction costs due to weight changes. Let $c$ denote the one-way transaction cost rate. The transaction cost factor is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mu_t = 1-c\sum_{i=1}^N \left|w_{i,t}-w^{drift}_{i,t}\right|.
\end{equation}
The portfolio value is updated as
\begin{equation}
V_{t+1}=V_t\cdot \mu_t \cdot (\mathbf{y}_t^\top \mathbf{w}_t).
\end{equation}

We adopt a hierarchical reward design. The Inner reward captures log-Alpha relative to the effective baseline:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{inn}=\log\frac{\mathbf{y}_t^\top \mathbf{w}_t}{\mathbf{y}_t^\top \mathbf{b}^{used}_t}.
\end{equation}
The Outer reward is evaluated only at rebalancing points and measures the improvement of risk-adjusted performance over a horizon $H$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
r_t^{\text{out}} = \mathbb{I}[g_t=1] \cdot \Big(
    &\mathrm{Sharpe}(\mathbf{b}_t^{\text{cand}}; t\!\to\!t+H) \\
    - &\mathrm{Sharpe}(\mathbf{b}_t^{\text{ref}}; t\!\to\!t+H)
\Big).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Finally, the learning objective is to maximize the expected weighted sum of hierarchical rewards
\begin{equation}
\max_{\theta_{out},\theta_{inn}} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_t r_t^{out}+\sum_t r_t^{inn}\right]
\end{equation}
subject to $\mathbf{w}_t,\mathbf{b}^{cand}_t\in\Delta^N$ and the rule-based gate $g_t=\mathrm{RuleGate}(p_t^{held})$.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Overall Framework and Decision Timing}
\label{sec:overall}

SentinelAlpha decomposes portfolio management into a three-stage pipeline:
\emph{probability modeling} $\rightarrow$ \emph{risk gating} $\rightarrow$ \emph{hierarchical execution}.
At each trading day close $t$, the system forms a closed-loop decision by reading two policy inputs:
a long-horizon state for low-frequency baseline allocation,
\begin{equation}
s_t^{out}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times \tau_{out}\times F},
\end{equation}
and a short-horizon state for high-frequency residual adjustment,
\begin{equation}
s_t^{inn}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times \tau_{inn}\times F}.
\end{equation}
It also observes the price-drifted exposure $w_t^{drift}$ and the drifted baseline $b_t^{drift}$.
Then, HD-SSM outputs an asset-wise uptrend probability $p_{i,t}$ and transition-related quantities,
which are aggregated into a portfolio-level risk signal.
A \emph{pure rule-based} sentinel (PHS) determines whether to trigger re-optimization:
if triggered, the Outer policy proposes a new baseline $b_t^{cand}$; otherwise, the system keeps $b_t^{drift}$.
Finally, the Inner policy outputs executed weights $w_t$ within a neighborhood of the chosen baseline $b_t^{used}$,
and the environment settles the close-to-close (C2C) return for $t\!\to\!t+1$.
This design decouples \emph{when to switch} from \emph{how to allocate}, improving responsiveness and stability under
regime shifts and tail-risk episodes.

\subsection{HD-SSM: Uptrend Regime Probability Modeling}
\label{sec:overall}
HD-SSM does not perform myopic next-day direction classification; instead, it estimates a continuous probability
flow of uptrend confidence
\begin{equation}
p_{i,t}\in(0,1),
\end{equation}
which is stable in smooth regimes yet sufficiently sensitive near turning points.
For asset $i$, the model encodes a causal feature window
\begin{equation}
X_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{W\times F}
\end{equation}
into local evidence $z_{i,t}$, and combines it with an asset embedding $e_i$ and a recurrent latent state $h_{i,t}$
to capture trend inertia, yielding $p_{i,t}$ after probability-space fusion.

\paragraph{Segment--Ensemble Soft Labels (Trend Probability Label).}
To reduce the noise of one-step direction labels, we construct soft targets from piecewise trend strength.
Given prices $\{P_t\}_{t=1}^T$, we apply log transform and smoothing:
\begin{equation}
x_t=\log(P_t+\epsilon),\qquad \tilde x^{(\theta)}=\mathrm{EMA}(x;\mathrm{span}=\theta_s).
\end{equation}
We segment $\tilde x^{(\theta)}$ by extrema and iteratively merge segments based on minimum length, slope similarity,
and shape similarity, obtaining a segment set $\mathcal S^{(\theta)}$.
For segment $m$ with slope $r_m$, we apply robust standardization (median/MAD) and map to probability:
\begin{equation}
z_m=\frac{r_m-\mathrm{median}(r)}{\mathrm{MAD}(r)+\epsilon},\qquad
y_m^{(\theta)}=\sigma(\alpha z_m).
\end{equation}
All timestamps within a segment share the segment-level probability. We ensemble over a parameter grid $\Theta$
and smooth the result to obtain the main label:
\begin{equation}
\bar y_{i,t}=\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta} y^{(\theta)}_{i,t},\qquad
y^{main}_{i,t}=\mathrm{Smooth}(\bar y_{i,t}).
\end{equation}
We additionally construct a short-horizon, high-sensitivity auxiliary label $y^{micro}_{i,t}$ to enhance local
turning-point sensitivity (e.g., using a shorter span and smaller minimum segment length, or a fast local mapping),
and use it to supervise the emission branch.

\paragraph{Model Architecture.} 
HD-SSM follows \emph{local evidence encoding + inertial recurrence + probability fusion}:
(i) a multi-scale residual TCN encoder maps $X_{i,t}$ to local evidence $z_{i,t}$;
(ii) a GRUCell maintains an inertial latent state $h_{i,t}$;
(iii) two lightweight heads output emission logits $\ell^{emit}_{i,t}$ and hazard transitions
$(q^{bear}_{i,t},q^{bull}_{i,t})$.
Formally,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\ell^{\mathrm{emit}}_{i,t} &= f_{\mathrm{emit}}\!\left([z_{i,t},\,e_i]\right),\\
\left(q^{\mathrm{bear}}_{i,t},\,q^{\mathrm{bull}}_{i,t}\right)
&= f_{\mathrm{trans}}\!\left([h_{i,t-1},\,z_{i,t},\,e_i]\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The inertial prior is updated by
\begin{equation}
p^{prior}_{i,t}=p_{i,t-1}(1-q^{bear}_{i,t})+(1-p_{i,t-1})q^{bull}_{i,t}
\end{equation}
and fused in logit space:
\begin{equation}
\ell^{fuse}_{i,t}=\operatorname{logit}(p^{prior}_{i,t})+\beta_i\,\ell^{emit}_{i,t}, \quad
p_{i,t}=\sigma(\ell^{fuse}_{i,t})
\end{equation}
where $\beta_i>0$ is an asset-wise learnable calibration coefficient.
The latent state is updated as
\begin{equation}
h_{i,t}=\operatorname{GRU}([z_{i,t},e_i],h_{i,t-1}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Training Loss.} 
HD-SSM jointly learns the final probability flow $p_{i,t}$, local emission evidence, and hazard transitions.
Given main soft targets $y^{main}_{i,t}$ and high-sensitivity auxiliary targets $y^{micro}_{i,t}$, the total loss is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SSM}}
&=\lambda_{\mathrm{fuse}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fuse}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{emit}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emit}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{deriv}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{deriv}}\\
&\quad
+\lambda_{\mathrm{macro}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{macro}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{refra}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{refra}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{tv}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tv}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\textbf{(1) Main probability fitting $\mathcal{L}_{fuse}$.}
We align fused logits $\ell^{fuse}_{i,t}$ to $y^{main}_{i,t}$ using a turning-point emphasized continuous focal-BCE:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fuse}}
&=\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\!\left[
w^{\mathrm{trend}}_{i,t}\cdot
\mathrm{CFocalBCE}\!\left(\ell^{\mathrm{fuse}}_{i,t},\,y^{\mathrm{main}}_{i,t}\right)
\right],\\
p_{i,t} &= \sigma\!\left(\ell^{\mathrm{fuse}}_{i,t}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\textbf{(2) Local evidence supervision $\mathcal{L}_{emit}$.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{emit}=\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\!\left[\mathrm{BCEWithLogits}(\ell^{emit}_{i,t},y^{micro}_{i,t})\right].
\end{equation}
\textbf{(3) Dynamic shape matching $\mathcal{L}_{deriv}$.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{deriv}=\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\!\left|\,(p_{i,t}-p_{i,t-1})-(y^{main}_{i,t}-y^{main}_{i,t-1})\,\right|.
\end{equation}
\textbf{(4) Macro inertial auxiliary $\mathcal{L}_{macro}$.}
Using the inertial state $h_{i,t-1}$, a macro head predicts the current trend:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{macro}}
&=\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\!\left[
\mathrm{BCEWithLogits}\!\left(\ell^{\mathrm{macro}}_{i,t},\,y^{\mathrm{main}}_{i,t}\right)
\right],\\
\ell^{\mathrm{macro}}_{i,t}
&= f_{\mathrm{macro}}\!\left(h_{i,t-1}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\textbf{(5) Refractory sparsity $\mathcal{L}_{refra}$.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{refra}=\mathcal{R}(q^{bear})+\mathcal{R}(q^{bull}).
\end{equation}
\textbf{(6) TV smoothing $\mathcal{L}_{tv}$.}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{tv}=\lambda_{tv}\,\mathbb{E}_{i,t}\big[|p_{i,t}-p_{i,t-1}|\big].
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{HD-SSM Training (Soft Labels $\rightarrow$ Probability Flow)}
\label{alg:ssm}
\algsmall
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

  \STATE Build training tuples $\big(X_{i,t-W:t},\,y^{\mathrm{main}}_{i,t},\,y^{\mathrm{micro}}_{i,t},\,i\big)$
\ENDFOR

\STATE Initialize encoder $f_{\mathrm{enc}}$, recurrent core (GRU), and heads $f_{\mathrm{emit}}, f_{\mathrm{trans}}$

\STATE \textbf{(C) Optimize probability flow.}
\FOR{$\mathrm{epoch}=1$ to $E$}

\subsection{Rule-based Sentinel (PHS)}
\label{sec:phs}

The gating layer is purely rule-based and does not receive gradient updates.
We compute a portfolio-level uptrend confidence by weighting asset probabilities with the drifted exposure:
\begin{equation}
p_t^{held}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{drift}_{i,t}\,p_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
To suppress one-day noise triggers, we maintain a low-probability counter:
\begin{equation}
c_t=
\begin{cases}
c_{t-1}+1,& p_t^{held}<\tau,\\
0,& p_t^{held}\ge \tau.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
A switch is triggered if the minimum holding constraint is satisfied and the counter reaches $m$; we also enforce
a hard maximum holding horizon:
\begin{equation}
g_t=\mathbf{1}\!\left[d_t\ge H_{max}\ \lor\ (d_t\ge H_{min}\land c_t\ge m)\right].
\end{equation}
This makes the risk boundary explicit and auditable, reducing high-variance and uncontrollable learned gating.

\subsection{HRL: Outer--Inner Hierarchical Policy Modeling}
\label{sec:hrl}

\paragraph{Outer (Low-frequency Beta Baseline)}
At decision points, the Outer policy proposes a candidate baseline. It extracts within-asset temporal
representations with LSTM-HA and models cross-asset dependencies via CAAN, then outputs Gaussian parameters and
samples continuous actions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a^{\mathrm{out}}_{i,t}
&\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_{i,t},\,\sigma_{i,t}^2\right),\\
\log \pi_{\mathrm{out}}\!\left(a_t^{\mathrm{out}} \mid s_t^{\mathrm{out}}\right)
&= \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log \mathcal{N}\!\left(a^{\mathrm{out}}_{i,t};
\mu_{i,t},\,\sigma_{i,t}^2\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We obtain a sparse candidate baseline by applying $\tilde a_t^{out}=\tanh(a_t^{out})$ and Top-$K$ softmax:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{cand}=\operatorname{TopKSoftmax}(\tilde a_t^{out}).
\end{equation}
The baseline used for execution is determined by the sentinel:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{used}=
\begin{cases}
b_t^{cand}, & g_t=1,\\
b_t^{drift}, & g_t=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Inner (High-frequency Residual Alpha).}
The Inner policy performs constrained micro-adjustments around $b_t^{used}$.
A lightweight causal dilated-convolution encoder extracts short-horizon dynamics and fuses $b_t^{used}$ with
$w_t^{drift}$. It samples continuous actions:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
a^{\mathrm{inn}}_{i,t}
&\sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu^{\mathrm{inn}}_{i,t},\,(\sigma^{\mathrm{inn}}_{i,t})^2\right),\\
\log \pi_{\mathrm{inn}}\!\left(a_t^{\mathrm{inn}} \mid s_t^{\mathrm{inn}}\right)
&= \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\log \mathcal{N}\!\left(
a^{\mathrm{inn}}_{i,t};
\mu^{\mathrm{inn}}_{i,t},\,(\sigma^{\mathrm{inn}}_{i,t})^2
\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
To re-rank only within the baseline support, we define a mask
\begin{equation}
m_{i,t}=\mathbf{1}[b^{used}_{i,t}>0],
\end{equation}
and apply a masked softmax to $\tilde a_t^{inn}=\tanh(a_t^{inn})$:
\begin{equation}
u_{i,t}=
\frac{m_{i,t}\exp(\tilde a^{inn}_{i,t})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_{j,t}\exp(\tilde a^{inn}_{j,t})}.
\end{equation}
The executed weights are obtained via a convex combination:
\begin{equation}
w_t=(1-\alpha)b_t^{used}+\alpha u_t,\qquad \alpha\in[0,1].
\end{equation}
This constraint substantially reduces the search space and focuses Inner on local re-ranking around the baseline.

\subsection{Algorithmic Procedure and Optimization}
\label{sec:opt}

\paragraph{C2C settlement, drift and transaction costs.}
We adopt close-to-close (C2C) settlement: at close $t$ we decide $w_t$ and settle on $t\to t+1$.
Let the relative price vector be
\begin{equation}
r_t=\left[\frac{P_{1,t+1}}{P_{1,t}},\dots,\frac{P_{N,t+1}}{P_{N,t}}\right]^\top .
\end{equation}
Because weights naturally drift with prices over $t-1\to t$, the realized exposure at $t$ before trading is
$w_t^{drift}$; similarly we obtain $b_t^{drift}$.
After trading at close $t$, the portfolio is adjusted from $w_t^{drift}$ to $w_t$.
With one-way fee rate $c$, turnover and cost are
\begin{equation}
\text{turnover}_t = \lVert w_t - w_t^{\text{drift}} \rVert_1, \qquad \text{cost}_t = c \, \text{turnover}_t.
\end{equation}
The net portfolio log return and the baseline log return are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R_t^{\mathrm{port}}
&= \log\!\left(
(\mathbf{w}_t^\top \mathbf{r}_t)\,
(1-\mathrm{cost}_t)
\right),\\
R_t^{\mathrm{base}}
&= \log\!\left(
(\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}})^\top \mathbf{r}_t
\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Hierarchical rewards.}
The Inner step reward measures residual alpha over the chosen baseline:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{inn}=R_t^{port}-R_t^{base}.
\end{equation}
Since the Outer action only takes effect when $g_t=1$, the Outer reward is defined only at switch points:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{out}=\mathbf{1}[g_t=1]\cdot\Big(\operatorname{Sharpe}(b_t^{used};H)-\operatorname{Sharpe}(b^{eq};H)\Big),
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Sharpe}(\cdot;H)$ is computed over the fixed window of length $H$ starting from the switch time.
In implementation, this corresponds to an event-level/segment-level return for low-frequency updates.

\paragraph{PPO objectives and masking.}
Both Inner and Outer are trained with the clipped PPO surrogate:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}^{x}_{\pi}
&= -\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[
\min\!\left(
\rho_t^x \hat A_t^x,\,
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\rho_t^x,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\right)
\hat A_t^x
\right)
\right],\\
\rho_t^x
&= \exp\!\left(
\log \pi_{\theta_x}(a_t \mid s_t)
- \log \pi^{\mathrm{old}}_{\theta_x}(a_t \mid s_t)
\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We jointly optimize policy and value with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^x=\mathcal{L}_\pi^x+\lambda_v\mathcal{L}_v^x-\lambda_e\mathcal{H}^x,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_v^x$ is the value regression loss and $\mathcal{H}^x$ is the entropy regularizer.
We apply action-semantic masking during updates: Outer is updated only on samples with $g_t=1$ (decision points),
while Inner is updated on all timesteps; we normalize $\hat A_t^{inn}$ to stabilize the ratio term.

\paragraph{Training schedule.}
We use a staged schedule \{warmup\_outer, warmup\_inner, joint\}: warmup phases decouple the two policies
(e.g., forcing $g_t=1$ for Outer and $g_t=0$ for Inner), followed by joint training with rule-based PHS gating to
reduce non-stationarity from multi-head coupling.


% ---------- Algorithm 2 (boxed) ----------
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{SentinelAlpha (Train Outer/Inner; Deploy with Rule-based PHS)}
\label{alg:sentinelalpha}
\algsmall
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Env; pretrained probability flow $\{p_{i,t}\}$; PPO hyper-parameters
\REQUIRE PHS parameters $(\tau,m,H_{\min},H_{\max})$
\ENSURE Learned $(\theta_{\mathrm{out}},\theta_{\mathrm{inn}})$; deployed policy (Outer + PHS + Inner)

\STATE \textbf{(A) Training (no learnable gate).}
\STATE Initialize $(\theta_{\mathrm{out}},\theta_{\mathrm{inn}})$ and buffers $(B_{\mathrm{out}},B_{\mathrm{inn}})$
\FORALL{$\mathrm{phase}\in\{\texttt{warmup\_out},\texttt{warmup\_inn},\texttt{joint}\}$}
  \FOR{$\mathrm{episode}=1$ to $N_{\mathrm{epi}}$}
    \STATE Reset Env; clear buffers
    \WHILE{not done}
      \STATE Observe $(s_t^{\mathrm{out}}, s_t^{\mathrm{inn}}, \mathbf{w}_t^{\mathrm{drift}}, \mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{drift}})$ and $\{p_{i,t}\}$
      \STATE Sample candidate baseline $\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{cand}} \sim \pi_{\mathrm{out}}(\cdot \mid s_t^{\mathrm{out}})$
      \STATE Set training gate $g_t$ by phase policy (force-switch / force-hold / joint)
      \IF{$g_t=1$}
        \STATE $\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}} \gets \mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{cand}}$
      \ELSE
        \STATE $\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}} \gets \mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{drift}}$
      \ENDIF
      \STATE Sample execution weights $\mathbf{w}_t \sim \pi_{\mathrm{inn}}(\cdot \mid s_t^{\mathrm{inn}},\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}},\mathbf{w}_t^{\mathrm{drift}})$
      \STATE Step Env; obtain $(r_t^{\mathrm{inn}}, r_t^{\mathrm{out}})$ and next state
      \STATE Store all steps in $B_{\mathrm{inn}}$; store decision steps in $B_{\mathrm{out}}$ with mask $g_t$
    \ENDWHILE
    \STATE Compute returns/advantages (GAE); PPO-update by phase; clear buffers
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR

\STATE \textbf{(B) Deployment / Backtest (enable PHS).}
\STATE Initialize counter $c\gets 0$ and holding days $d\gets 0$
\FOR{$t=0$ to $T-1$}
  \STATE $p_t^{\mathrm{held}} \gets \sum_i w^{\mathrm{drift}}_{i,t}\,p_{i,t}$
  \IF{$p_t^{\mathrm{held}}<\tau$}
    \STATE $c \gets c+1$
  \ELSE
    \STATE $c \gets 0$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $\text{cond}_1 \gets (d \ge H_{\max})$
  \STATE $\text{cond}_2 \gets (d \ge H_{\min}) \land (c \ge m)$
  \STATE $g_t \gets \one[\text{cond}_1 \lor \text{cond}_2]$
  \STATE $\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{cand}} \gets \pi_{\mathrm{out}}(s_t^{\mathrm{out}},\mathbf{w}_t^{\mathrm{drift}})$
  \IF{$g_t=1$}
    \STATE $\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}} \gets \mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{cand}}$; $d \gets 1$
  \ELSE
    \STATE $\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}} \gets \mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{drift}}$; $d \gets d+1$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $\mathbf{w}_t \gets \pi_{\mathrm{inn}}(s_t^{\mathrm{inn}},\mathbf{b}_t^{\mathrm{used}},\mathbf{w}_t^{\mathrm{drift}})$
  \STATE Env.step$(\mathbf{w}_t)$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Baseline Methods}

To comprehensively evaluate \textbf{SentinelAlpha}, we consider three categories of baselines: 
(1) traditional and online portfolio selection methods, 
(2) deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods, and 
(3) structural ablations of our model. 

This grouping covers passive investment, mean--variance optimization, mean reversion, online rebalancing, and deep RL-based portfolio generation, enabling a structured analysis of performance sources.

\paragraph{Traditional and Online Portfolio Selection}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Buy\&Hold}: Uniform buy-and-hold without active rebalancing, representing passive market exposure.
    
    \item \textbf{Markowitz}: Classical mean--variance optimization balancing expected return and variance, serving as a representative of modern portfolio theory.
    
    \item \textbf{UCRP (Uniform Constant Rebalanced Portfolio)}: Equal-weight constant rebalancing strategy, a strong baseline in online portfolio selection.
    
    \item \textbf{Anticor}: An online strategy exploiting historical correlation/anti-correlation structures for weight transfer, emphasizing cross-asset relationships.
    
    \item \textbf{OLMAR}: Online Moving Average Reversion, based on the moving-average mean-reversion hypothesis.
    
    \item \textbf{WMAMR}: Weighted moving-average mean-reversion strategy, introducing stronger smoothing mechanisms on top of OLMAR.
\end{itemize}

These baselines collectively represent passive holding, mean--variance optimization, online rebalancing, and mean-reversion paradigms.

\paragraph{Deep Reinforcement Learning Baselines}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AlphaStock}: A DRL-based portfolio method employing temporal representation networks (LSTM-HA) and cross-asset attention (CAAN), optimized under risk-adjusted objectives such as the Sharpe ratio. It represents the ``cross-asset attention + risk-aware RL'' paradigm.
    
    \item \textbf{DeepTrader}: A hierarchical DRL framework incorporating both asset-level and market-level scoring units, emphasizing risk--return balance and market-state adaptation. Its structure aligns conceptually with regime-aware decision-making.
\end{itemize}

These DRL baselines represent two mainstream approaches: 
(1) cross-asset attention-driven portfolio generation (AlphaStock), and 
(2) market-state-aware risk modulation (DeepTrader).

\paragraph{Ablation Variants of SentinelAlpha}

To analyze the contribution of each module, we construct structural ablations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Outer-only}: Retains only the low-frequency Outer policy, evaluating standalone baseline allocation ability.
    
    \item \textbf{Outer\&Inner}: Adds the Inner execution layer to assess the marginal contribution of short-horizon reweighting.
    
    \item \textbf{Outer\&Inner\&Monitor}: The full SentinelAlpha model with rule-based risk gating.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate investment performance from three perspectives: \emph{profit}, \emph{risk}, and \emph{risk-adjusted return}. All metrics are computed under the \textbf{Close-to-Close (C2C)} protocol, where positions are formed at market close $t$ and evaluated using returns from $t$ to $t+1$.

Let $\{V_t\}_{t=0}^{T}$ denote the portfolio value series. Daily return is defined as
\begin{equation}
r_t = \frac{V_t - V_{t-1}}{V_{t-1}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(1) Total Return (TR).}
\begin{equation}
\text{TR} = \frac{V_T}{V_0} - 1.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(2) Annualized Return (ARR).}
Let $\bar r = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t$. Then
\begin{equation}
\text{ARR} = (1 + \bar r)^{252} - 1.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(3) Annualized Volatility (AVOL).}
\begin{equation}
\text{AVOL} = \sigma(r)\sqrt{252},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(r)$ denotes the standard deviation of daily returns.

\paragraph{(4) Maximum Drawdown (MDD).}
\begin{equation}
\text{MDD} = 
\max_{t \le T}
\left(
1 - \frac{V_t}{\max_{s \le t} V_s}
\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{(5) Annualized Sharpe Ratio (ASR).}
Assuming zero risk-free rate:
\begin{equation}
\text{ASR} = 
\frac{\bar r}{\sigma(r)+\epsilon}\sqrt{252},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a numerical stability constant.

\paragraph{(6) Calmar Ratio (CR).}
\begin{equation}
\text{CR} = 
\frac{\text{ARR}}{\text{MDD}+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}

Sharpe emphasizes return per unit volatility, whereas Calmar highlights return relative to extreme downside risk, making it particularly suitable for risk-aware portfolio evaluation.

\section{Discussions and Conclusions}
This paper proposes \textbf{SentinelAlpha}, a sentinel-driven regime-aware hierarchical reinforcement learning framework designed for non-stationary financial markets. By structurally decoupling 
(i) \emph{SSM-based probabilistic regime modeling} (market and asset state perception), 
(ii) an \emph{Outer--Inner hierarchical strategy} (low-frequency allocation with short-term execution refinement), and 
(iii) an \emph{exogenous risk gating mechanism} (Sentinel), 
the framework establishes a portfolio management paradigm that prioritizes \emph{risk supervision before return optimization}.

Empirical comparisons against traditional investment strategies, online portfolio selection methods, and representative DRL baselines (e.g., AlphaStock and DeepTrader) demonstrate that SentinelAlpha achieves superior or more robust risk--return trade-offs in both U.S. and Chinese equity markets. In particular, the model consistently exhibits advantages in Sharpe ratio, Calmar ratio, and maximum drawdown control. 

Ablation studies further confirm that the sentinel-based risk gating module serves as the primary driver of performance improvement, while the Inner execution layer provides stable marginal gains when coordinated with the Outer allocation layer and the gating mechanism.

Overall, integrating regime-aware probability flows, hierarchical policy learning, and interpretable risk constraints within a unified framework proves to be an effective approach for enhancing portfolio robustness and cross-market generalization.

Future work will explore hybrid designs that combine learnable and rule-based gating mechanisms, incorporate more realistic transaction cost and market impact models, and extend robustness validation to broader markets and longer rolling horizons.

\subsection{Paper Size, Margins, and Column Width}
Papers must be formatted to print in two-column format on 8.5 x 11 inch US letter-sized paper. The margins must be exactly as follows:

\item Left margin: .75 inches
\item Right margin: .75 inches
\item Bottom margin: 1.25 inches

\subsubsection{Column Width and Margins.}
To ensure maximum readability, your paper must include two columns. Each column should be 3.3 inches wide (slightly more than 3.25 inches), with a .375 inch (.952 cm) gutter of white space between the two columns. The aaai2026.sty file will automatically create these columns for you.

\subsection{Overlength Papers}
If your paper is too long and you resort to formatting tricks to make it fit, it is quite likely that it will be returned to you. The best way to retain readability if the paper is overlength is to cut text, figures, or tables. There are a few acceptable ways to reduce paper size that don't affect readability. First, turn on \textbackslash frenchspacing, which will reduce the space after periods. Next, move all your figures and tables to the top of the page. Consider removing less important portions of a figure. If you use \textbackslash centering instead of \textbackslash begin\{center\} in your figure environment, you can also buy some space. For mathematical environments, you may reduce fontsize {\bf but not below 6.5 point}.


Commands that alter page layout are forbidden. These include \textbackslash columnsep,  \textbackslash float, \textbackslash topmargin, \textbackslash topskip, \textbackslash textheight, \textbackslash textwidth, \textbackslash oddsidemargin, and \textbackslash evensizemargin (this list is not exhaustive). If you alter page layout, you will be required to pay the page fee. Other commands that are questionable and may cause your paper to be rejected include \textbackslash parindent, and \textbackslash parskip. Commands that alter the space between sections are forbidden. The title sec package is not allowed. Regardless of the above, if your paper is obviously ``squeezed" it is not going to to be accepted. Options for reducing the length of a paper include reducing the size of your graphics, cutting text, or paying the extra page charge (if it is offered).


\subsection{Type Font and Size}
Your paper must be formatted in Times Roman or Nimbus. We will not accept papers formatted using Computer Modern or Palatino or some other font as the text or heading typeface. Sans serif, when used, should be Courier. Use Symbol or Lucida or Computer Modern for \textit{mathematics only. }

Do not use type 3 fonts for any portion of your paper, including graphics. Type 3 bitmapped fonts are designed for fixed resolution printers. Most print at 300 dpi even if the printer resolution is 1200 dpi or higher. They also often cause high resolution imagesetter devices to crash. Consequently, AAAI will not accept electronic files containing obsolete type 3 fonts. Files containing those fonts (even in graphics) will be rejected. (Authors using blackboard symbols must avoid packages that use type 3 fonts.)

Fortunately, there are effective workarounds that will prevent your file from embedding type 3 bitmapped fonts. The easiest workaround is to use the required times, helvet, and courier packages with \LaTeX{}2e. (Note that papers formatted in this way will still use Computer Modern for the mathematics. To make the math look good, you'll either have to use Symbol or Lucida, or you will need to install type 1 Computer Modern fonts --- for more on these fonts, see the section ``Obtaining Type 1 Computer Modern.")

If you are unsure if your paper contains type 3 fonts, view the PDF in Acrobat Reader. The Properties/Fonts window will display the font name, font type, and encoding properties of all the fonts in the document. If you are unsure if your graphics contain type 3 fonts (and they are PostScript or encapsulated PostScript documents), create PDF versions of them, and consult the properties window in Acrobat Reader.

The default size for your type must be ten-point with twelve-point leading (line spacing). Start all pages (except the first) directly under the top margin. (See the next section for instructions on formatting the title page.) Indent ten points when beginning a new paragraph, unless the paragraph begins directly below a heading or subheading.

\subsection{Title and Authors}
Your title must appear centered over both text columns in sixteen-point bold type (twenty-four point leading). The title must be written in Title Case according to the Chicago Manual of Style rules. The rules are a bit involved, but in general verbs (including short verbs like be, is, using, and go), nouns, adverbs, adjectives, and pronouns should be capitalized, (including both words in hyphenated terms), while articles, conjunctions, and prepositions are lower case unless they directly follow a colon or long dash. You can use the online tool \url{https://titlecaseconverter.com/} to double-check the proper capitalization (select the "Chicago" style and mark the "Show explanations" checkbox).

Author's names should appear below the title of the paper, centered in twelve-point type (with fifteen point leading), along with affiliation(s) and complete address(es) (including electronic mail address if available) in nine-point roman type (the twelve point leading). You should begin the two-column format when you come to the abstract.

\subsection{Tables}

Tables should be presented in 10 point roman type. If necessary, they may be altered to 9 point type. You must not use \texttt{\textbackslash resizebox} or other commands that resize the entire table to make it smaller, because you can't control the final font size this way.
If your table is too large you can use \texttt{\textbackslash setlength\{\textbackslash tabcolsep\}\{1mm\}} to compress the columns a bit or you can adapt the content (e.g.: reduce the decimal precision when presenting numbers, use shortened column titles, make some column duble-line to get it narrower).

Tables that do not fit in a single column must be placed across double columns. If your table won't fit within the margins even when spanning both columns and using the above techniques, you must split it in two separate tables.

\subsubsection {Table Captions.} The number and caption for your table must appear \textit{under} (not above) the table.  Additionally, the font and size of table captions must be 10 point roman and must be placed beneath the figure. Do not make them smaller, bold, or italic. (Individual words may be italicized if the context requires differentiation.)



\subsubsection{Low-Resolution Bitmaps.}
You may not use low-resolution (such as 72 dpi) screen-dumps and GIF files---these files contain so few pixels that they are always blurry, and illegible when printed. If they are color, they will become an indecipherable mess when converted to black and white. This is always the case with gif files, which should never be used. The resolution of screen dumps can be increased by reducing the print size of the original file while retaining the same number of pixels. You can also enlarge files by manipulating them in software such as PhotoShop. Your figures should be 300 dpi when incorporated into your document.

\subsubsection{\LaTeX{} Overflow.}
\LaTeX{} users please beware: \LaTeX{} will sometimes put portions of the figure or table or an equation in the margin. If this happens, you need to make the figure or table span both columns. If absolutely necessary, you may reduce the figure, or reformat the equation, or reconfigure the table.{ \bf Check your log file!} You must fix any overflow into the margin (that means no overfull boxes in \LaTeX{}). \textbf{Nothing is permitted to intrude into the margin or gutter.}


\subsubsection{Using Color.}
Use of color is restricted to figures only. It must be WACG 2.0 compliant. (That is, the contrast ratio must be greater than 4.5:1 no matter the font size.) It must be CMYK, NOT RGB. It may never be used for any portion of the text of your paper. The archival version of your paper will be printed in black and white and grayscale. The web version must be readable by persons with disabilities. Consequently, because conversion to grayscale can cause undesirable effects (red changes to black, yellow can disappear, and so forth), we strongly suggest you avoid placing color figures in your document. If you do include color figures, you must (1) use the CMYK (not RGB) colorspace and (2) be mindful of readers who may happen to have trouble distinguishing colors. Your paper must be decipherable without using color for distinction.

\subsubsection{Drawings.}
We suggest you use computer drawing software (such as Adobe Illustrator or, (if unavoidable), the drawing tools in Microsoft Word) to create your illustrations. Do not use Microsoft Publisher. These illustrations will look best if all line widths are uniform (half- to two-point in size), and you do not create labels over shaded areas. Shading should be 133 lines per inch if possible. Use Times Roman or Helvetica for all figure call-outs. \textbf{Do not use hairline width lines} --- be sure that the stroke width of all lines is at least .5 pt. Zero point lines will print on a laser printer, but will completely disappear on the high-resolution devices used by our printers.

\subsubsection{Photographs and Images.}
Photographs and other images should be in grayscale (color photographs will not reproduce well; for example, red tones will reproduce as black, yellow may turn to white, and so forth) and set to a minimum of 300 dpi. Do not prescreen images.

\subsubsection{Resizing Graphics.}
Resize your graphics \textbf{before} you include them with LaTeX. You may \textbf{not} use trim or clip options as part of your \textbackslash includegraphics command. Resize the media box of your PDF using a graphics program instead.

\subsubsection{Fonts in Your Illustrations.}
You must embed all fonts in your graphics before including them in your LaTeX document.

\subsubsection{Algorithms.}
Algorithms and/or programs are a special kind of figures. Like all illustrations, they should appear floated to the top (preferably) or bottom of the page. However, their caption should appear in the header, left-justified and enclosed between horizontal lines, as shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:algorithm}. The algorithm body should be terminated with another horizontal line. It is up to the authors to decide whether to show line numbers or not, how to format comments, etc.

In \LaTeX{} algorithms may be typeset using the {\tt algorithm} and {\tt algorithmic} packages, but you can also use one of the many other packages for the task.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Example algorithm}
\label{alg:algorithm}
\textbf{Input}: Your algorithm's input\\
\textbf{Parameter}: Optional list of parameters\\
\textbf{Output}: Your algorithm's output
\begin{algorithmic}[1] %[1] enables line numbers
\STATE Let $t=0$.
\WHILE{condition}
\STATE Do some action.
\IF {conditional}
\STATE Perform task A.
\ELSE
\STATE Perform task B.
\ENDIF
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \textbf{return} solution
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Listings.}
Listings are much like algorithms and programs. They should also appear floated to the top (preferably) or bottom of the page. Listing captions should appear in the header, left-justified and enclosed between horizontal lines as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:listing}. Terminate the body with another horizontal line and avoid any background color. Line numbers, if included, must appear within the text column.

\begin{listing}[tb]%
\caption{Example listing {\tt quicksort.hs}}%
\label{lst:listing}%
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Haskell]
quicksort :: Ord a => [a] -> [a]
quicksort []     = []
quicksort (p:xs) = (quicksort lesser) ++ [p] ++ (quicksort greater)
	where
		lesser  = filter (< p) xs
		greater = filter (>= p) xs
\end{lstlisting}
\end{listing}

\subsection{References}
The AAAI style includes a set of definitions for use in formatting references with BibTeX. These definitions make the bibliography style fairly close to the ones  specified in the Reference Examples appendix below. To use these definitions, you also need the BibTeX style file ``aaai2026.bst," available in the AAAI Author Kit on the AAAI web site. Then, at the end of your paper but before \textbackslash end{document}, you need to put the following lines:

\begin{quote}
\begin{small}
\textbackslash bibliography\{bibfile1,bibfile2,...\}
\end{small}
\end{quote}

Please note that the aaai2026.sty class already sets the bibliographystyle for you, so you do not have to place any \textbackslash bibliographystyle command in the document yourselves. The aaai2026.sty file is incompatible with the hyperref and navigator packages. If you use either, your references will be garbled and your paper will be returned to you.

References may be the same size as surrounding text.
However, in this section (only), you may reduce the size to {\em \textbackslash small} (9pt) if your paper exceeds the allowable number of pages. Making it any smaller than 9 point with 10 point linespacing, however, is not allowed.

The list of files in the \textbackslash bibliography command should be the names of your BibTeX source files (that is, the .bib files referenced in your paper).

The following commands are available for your use in citing references:
\begin{quote}
{\em \textbackslash cite:} Cites the given reference(s) with a full citation. This appears as ``(Author Year)'' for one reference, or ``(Author Year; Author Year)'' for multiple references.\smallskip\\
{\em \textbackslash shortcite:} Cites the given reference(s) with just the year. This appears as ``(Year)'' for one reference, or ``(Year; Year)'' for multiple references.\smallskip\\
{\em \textbackslash citeauthor:} Cites the given reference(s) with just the author name(s) and no parentheses.\smallskip\\
{\em \textbackslash citeyear:} Cites the given reference(s) with just the date(s) and no parentheses.
\end{quote}
You may also use any of the \emph{natbib} citation commands.

\appendix
\section{Reference Examples}
\label{sec:reference_examples}

\nobibliography*
Formatted bibliographies should look like the following examples. You should use BibTeX to generate the references. Missing fields are unacceptable when compiling references, and usually indicate that you are using the wrong type of entry (BibTeX class).

\paragraph{Book with multiple authors~\nocite{em:86}} Use the \texttt{@book} class.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{em:86}.

\paragraph{Journal and magazine articles~\nocite{r:80, hcr:83}} Use the \texttt{@article} class.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{r:80}.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{hcr:83}.

\paragraph{Proceedings paper published by a society, press or publisher~\nocite{c:83, c:84}} Use the \texttt{@inproceedings} class. You may abbreviate the \emph{booktitle} field, but make sure that the conference edition is clear.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{c:84}.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{c:83}.

\paragraph{University technical report~\nocite{r:86}} Use the \texttt{@techreport} class.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{r:86}.

\paragraph{Dissertation or thesis~\nocite{c:79}} Use the \texttt{@phdthesis} class.\\[.2em]
\bibentry{c:79}.

\paragraph{Forthcoming publication~\nocite{c:21}} Use the \texttt{@misc} class with a \texttt{note="Forthcoming"} annotation.
\begin{quote}
\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
@misc(key,
  [...]
  note="Forthcoming",
)
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}
\end{quote}
\bibentry{c:21}.

\paragraph{ArXiv paper~\nocite{c:22}} Fetch the BibTeX entry from the "Export Bibtex Citation" link in the arXiv website. Notice it uses the \texttt{@misc} class instead of the \texttt{@article} one, and that it includes the \texttt{eprint} and \texttt{archivePrefix} keys.
\begin{quote}
\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
@misc(key,
  [...]
  eprint="xxxx.yyyy",
  archivePrefix="arXiv",
)
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}
\end{quote}
\bibentry{c:22}.

\paragraph{Website or online resource~\nocite{c:23}} Use the \texttt{@misc} class. Add the url in the \texttt{howpublished} field and the date of access in the \texttt{note} field:
\begin{quote}
\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{verbatim}
@misc(key,
  [...]
  howpublished="\url{http://...}",
  note="Accessed: YYYY-mm-dd",
)
\end{verbatim}
\end{footnotesize}
\end{quote}
\bibentry{c:23}.
